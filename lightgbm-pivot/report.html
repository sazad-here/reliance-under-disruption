Stockout model pivot: PatchTST out, LightGBM in

We validated LightGBM against published results on four public datasets: Kaggle backorders, Walmart M5 (where it would have ranked 39th of 5,507 teams), DataCo and Olist. We then retrained it on the study's simulator with every one of the 70 demand series held out once. It also compares ten models at 50 and 100 training epochs. This page records the decision, the numbers, and what can and cannot be claimed.

The decision

Why the model changed

PatchTST, and its fusion with a second Transformer, never beat simple models on this task. Across ten architectures it finished last (AUROC 0.859), and a logistic regression on one hand-computed number, net inventory position, matched it exactly. Whether a stockout happens in the next two weeks is decided by the current stock position plus future randomness. There is no long-range pattern for a time-series Transformer to find.

The AI component is now a gradient-boosted tree model: the family that wins at this data scale (Grinsztajn et al., NeurIPS 2022), that the anchor paper also tested, and whose behaviour can be explained to reviewers and participants. PatchTST code and results are archived in archive/patchtst/; nothing was deleted.

Part 1 · External validation

Benchmark against a published paper

Ntakolia, Kokkotis, Karlsson & Moustakidis (2021), An Explainable Machine Learning Model for Material Backorder Prediction in Inventory Management, Sensors 21(23), 7926. Public Kaggle data: 1,929,935 SKUs, 13,981 (0.72 %) went on backorder. Their protocol, rebuilt: combine the two Kaggle files, undersample to 1:1, 80/20 split. Our test set has 5,593 rows; theirs has 5,588.

What made the difference

The paper balances the data by discarding about 1.9 million on-time SKUs. Model E trains five extra LightGBM models, each on the same backorder rows plus a fresh sample of those discarded rows, and averages them (EasyEnsemble; Liu, Wu & Zhou 2009). Test rows never enter a bag; the script asserts it. Extra features and stacking with logistic regression added nothing, so the final model is LightGBM alone.

Claim the paired gain, not the headline gap. The paper's own LightGBM, re-run, already scores 90.30 %. Most of the 2.9-point gap to their printed 87.78 % is reproduction noise, and a reviewer who re-runs it will see that. The defensible improvement is +0.34 pt accuracy and +0.005 AUROC over a faithful reproduction, on 5 of 5 seeds.

“Reproducing the protocol of Ntakolia et al. (2021) on the public Kaggle backorder dataset, our LightGBM baseline reached 90.30 ± 0.42 % accuracy (AUROC 0.958) over five random splits, against 87.78 % reported. Training the same model as an EasyEnsemble over the majority-class records that undersampling discards raised this to 90.65 ± 0.49 % (AUROC 0.9635 ± 0.0019), improving on the reproduced baseline on all five splits.”
Part 2 · The datasets behind the simulator

LightGBM on Walmart M5, DataCo and Olist

The simulator's demand comes from Walmart's M5 data, its disruption rate from DataCo and its lead times from Olist. Each dataset was run against a published result, under that paper's own protocol, before any comparison was made.

Walmart M5: the M5 Accuracy competition

Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos (2022), International Journal of Forecasting 38(4). This is the official evaluation phase: train through day 1941, forecast days 1942–1969 for all 30,490 products, and score by WRMSSE over 42,840 series at 12 aggregation levels. The hidden test file and the weights come from the organisers' own release. Before training, our scorer reproduced two published scores exactly, ES_bu 0.670981 and seasonal naïve 0.847017, so our number is the leaderboard's number.

The model would have ranked 39th of 5,507 teams (top 1 %). Every tuning choice used days 1914–1941 only. One earlier run was stopped before it produced a test score, because it had chosen too few boosting rounds; that decision was made on the validation window, and it's recorded in the script.

DataCo and Olist: Li (2026), shipment prioritisation

Li (2026), arXiv:2607.18573, AIIIP 2026, takes the leakage-controlled approach DataCo needs: only features known at purchase, five time-ordered folds. Its question is operational: reviewing only 10 % of orders, how much delay exposure (days late × order value) does ranking by a model's predictions catch, compared with simply checking the most valuable orders first (VALUE_ONLY)? We rebuilt the data exactly (Olist 96,470 orders, 8.11 % late, mean value 137.04; DataCo 103,400 late rows). We ran their XGBoost with their settings on the GPU, then LightGBM on the identical features and folds.

  • DataCo: replicated, and LightGBM ties XGBoost. Our XGBoost gives .322 where the paper reports .323; LightGBM is identical (difference +.000, CI ±.001). Both beat value sorting by 10 points, as the paper found.
  • Olist: we beat the paper, and so does our XGBoost. The paper's model loses to value sorting (−4.9 points). With our purchase-time features (seller and customer location, their distance, the promised delivery window, payment details), XGBoost beats value sorting by +4.1 points, CI clear of zero.   • What this means for the study: LightGBM is competitive everywhere (top 1 % on M5, level on DataCo), but it doesn't win everywhere. On Olist, XGBoost with the paper's conservative settings (180 shallow trees) handled the drift between time blocks better.

How to cite these. M5 is the strongest result: an official leaderboard, a verified scorer and a held-out test. The DataCo comparison is a clean replication. The Olist gain is real but single-run and comes from feature engineering, so report it as “richer purchase-time features let a model clear the value-only gate on Olist, reversing the paper's conclusion”. It's not a LightGBM win.

How to read this

  • The model works. AUROC 0.891 on series it has never seen, and 86.2 % accuracy against a 78.5 % majority floor, with calibration error under 0.01. That is the reliability anchor the study needs.
  • Model families are tied here. The largest AUROC difference is 0.002, statistically detectable but far too small to matter. On the simulator the stockout is mostly decided by the current stock position, so the ceiling is set by the task, not the model.
  • EasyEnsemble doesn't carry over, as predicted. It won on Kaggle by recovering rows that undersampling threw away. The simulator discards nothing, so the bags only reshuffle data the plain model already uses.
  • Why LightGBM, not XGBoost. XGBoost is 0.002 AUROC higher on the simulator, but a full point lower on the external benchmark (89.63 % vs 90.65 %). The model we cite as validated should be the one that was validated.

About the GPU run. The installed LightGBM was built without GPU support, and rebuilding it needs the CUDA toolkit and CMake, neither of which is on this machine. XGBoost ran on the RTX 4060 (CUDA confirmed per fit; about 45 % utilisation in bursts). With 25k rows the GPU gives no speed advantage: 7 s vs 6 s for LightGBM on CPU across all five folds. The recommended model needs no GPU.

Part 4 · Updated 25 September

Ten models, 50 vs 100 epochs

Every model was trained on the same simulator data and scored on identical test rows, with 5-fold cross-validation across all 70 demand series. Neural models trained a fixed 100 epochs and were scored at epoch 50, at epoch 100, and at the epoch the validation data picks. For boosted trees, the equivalent is 50 trees, 100 trees, and the early-stopped count.

  • LightGBM is top tier, not the outright winner. The top four models are within 0.002 AUROC. The stockout is mostly decided by the current stock position, so the task sets the ceiling.
  • It is significantly better than six deep-learning models, including PatchTST by 0.034 (series-clustered 95 % intervals).
  • Longer training made most neural models worse: CNN 0.871 → 0.832, JointPatchTransformer 0.880 → 0.849, PatchTST 0.846 → 0.827. That is overfitting; their best epochs were mostly 6–23. LightGBM improved from 50 to 100 trees and did not overfit.

The model participants actually see

A session lasts only 35 weeks, so the benchmarked model's 48-week features do not exist live. The live LightGBM uses the current state plus four weeks of history. It reaches AUROC 0.897 (cross-validated by series), 0.902 on the demand participants play (never trained on), and calibration error 0.006, so a displayed 30 % means about 30 %. The recommended orders stay scripted so reliability is exactly 80 % or 60 %. On error weeks the risk is computed from the stock level the AI misreads, so wrong advice looks like a real model's mistake.

How to write it. "LightGBM performed in the top tier (AUROC 0.891), statistically equivalent to logistic regression and XGBoost and significantly better than six deep architectures including PatchTST; it was chosen for its external validation, calibration and speed in live use." Do not write that it outperformed all models.

Caution

The benchmark we rejected

Ahmed et al. (2025), Scientific Reports reports 96 % on DataCo late-delivery prediction with SOM+ANN. DataCo's late-delivery label is simply “real shipping days > scheduled days” (that rule reproduces 97.6 % of labels), and the paper feeds the real-shipping-days column, known only after delivery, in as an input.

Beating 96 % there is trivial and meaningless. It is worth one sentence in the paper as a caution about that dataset.

Disclosure

Caveats

  • The anchor paper states no random seed, so its exact split cannot be recreated. Hence five seeds and paired differences.
  • We used the paper's selected hyper-parameters (LightGBM: 520 trees, 50 leaves; XGBoost: depth 9, γ 0.7), not its grid search.
  • Benchmark accuracy is at a 0.5 threshold on a balanced test set, as in the paper. On real data (0.72 % positive), AUROC and calibration matter more.
  • EasyEnsemble probabilities are averaged over balanced bags and are not calibrated to the real base rate. The simulator models were Platt-calibrated before scoring; keep that step for anything shown to participants (van den Goorbergh et al., JAMIA 2022).
  • On the simulator, logistic regression is marginally better on PR-AUC. Report it; it strengthens the point that the task, not the architecture, sets the ceiling.
