About this study: please read

Thank you for taking part. Now that you have finished, here is what the study was really testing, and some details we could not tell you at the start.

The assistant's accuracy was set in advance

The stockout-risk estimates you saw came from a real forecasting model running on your warehouse. The recommended orders, however, followed a fixed schedule that decided in advance which weeks the assistant would get right, and on the weeks it was wrong, its risk estimate was worked out from the same mistaken reading of your stock. In your session the assistant was right on of weeks (%). Other participants saw an assistant that was right % of the time. Which version you got was decided only by the order in which people arrived.

Where the mistakes fell was also planned

Of the assistant's wrong recommendations, were placed in weeks where a bad order does lasting damage, mostly in the run-up to a disruption, and fell in calm weeks, when it is easily absorbed. We are studying whether people rely on an assistant differently depending on when it errs, not just how often.

Why this was not explained earlier

If people know an assistant's exact accuracy, or that its errors are planned, they use it differently from how they would use a real tool. So that information had to be held back until now. Nothing you were told during the study was false. Your own orders, not the assistant's, determined your warehouse's results, and those results have no consequences for you.

Why this matters

Supply-chain planners increasingly work alongside AI tools that forecast demand, estimate the risk of running out and recommend orders. No such tool is right every time, and its mistakes do not all cost the same. Some of the costliest are made just before a disruption, such as a supplier problem or a transport delay, because a shortfall ordered then carries over into the weeks that follow.

Working well with such a tool means knowing when to rely on it and when to question it. Researchers call this appropriate reliance, and it is what this study measures. Your decisions help show how people respond to an assistant whose mistakes fall at different moments, which can help design AI tools that support people better.
