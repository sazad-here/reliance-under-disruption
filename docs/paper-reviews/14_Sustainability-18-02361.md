## 1 SNAPSHOT
- **Paper**: Liu, C., Wang, Y., Purvis, L., Potter, A. "Can Digital Twin Technology Enhance Supply-Chain Resilience? A Systematic Literature Review." *Sustainability* 2026, 18(5), 2361.
- **Type**: PRISMA-aligned systematic literature review. No new empirical data.
- **Corpus**: 89 peer-reviewed articles from Scopus + Web of Science, search date 31 May 2025, no year restriction, English only.
- **Contribution claimed**: a *stage-based* synthesis mapping digital-twin capabilities onto four resilience stages — preparedness, resistance, rebound, **growth** — with capabilities kept conceptually separate from benefits and from adoption barriers.
- **Secondary framing**: a dynamic-capability-view (DCV) interpretation (Fig. 9) aligning sensing → preparedness, seizing → resistance, transforming → rebound and growth.

## 2 ABSTRACT
- **Claim**: "prior evidence is fragmented and lacks an integrated view across disruption stages."
- **Claim**: 89 articles reviewed with "relevance-based screening to retain studies with substantive theoretical and practical implications."
- **Claim**: DTT contribution "differs by resilience stage", with the four-stage capability list quoted in the scratchpad.
- **Claim**: barriers are "data quality limitations, high implementation costs, shortages of specialised skills, and governance challenges".
- **[Assessment]** The abstract is descriptive and hedged ("indicates", "suggests"); it claims no measured effect. The one place it overstates is "shortages of specialised skills" and "governance challenges" as *key* barriers — neither appears as a row in Table 7, the paper's own barrier tabulation (which lists interoperability, data accuracy, cost, security/privacy, model complexity, connectivity, system compatibility). Skills and governance appear only in the narrative of §5.3–5.4.

## 3 INTRODUCTION AND MOTIVATION
- Motivation: volatility and disruption have made supply-chain resilience "a strategic priority"; digital twins are increasingly proposed as a response; existing reviews cover DTT concepts and enabling technologies but not the resilience linkage.
- Three explicit gaps (scratchpad): capabilities not specified as mechanisms; no temporal/stage clarity; fragmented adoption evidence.
- Positioning (§1 p.2): "this review contributes a stage-based synthesis that maps DTT capabilities onto four resilience stages… and makes the coding logic explicit. We distinguish DTT capabilities from performance benefits and adoption barriers, and we transparently separate the broader evidence base from the subset of studies that provide sufficient stage relevant detail for classification."
- **[Assessment]** The three-way separation of *capability / benefit / barrier* is genuinely useful and is the paper's clearest methodological idea. Whether it is executed cleanly is a separate question (see §7 and §12).

## 4 LITERATURE REVIEW / RELATED WORK
- §2.1 defines the digital twin and traces nine definitions from 2010 to 2022 (Table 1), then decomposes DTT into four technology layers: data acquisition and transmission (IoT/sensors); data storage and processing (cloud/edge, big-data analytics); **modelling and simulation** ("3D modelling and simulation… enable what-if scenario analysis… More recently, the use of machine learning and AI is increasingly utilised to predict the future behaviour of the target systems"); and visualisation and interaction (dashboards, AR).
- §2.2 adopts a two-level structure — network level (multi-stakeholder value network) and site level (warehouses, production facilities) — attributed to [22].
- §2.3 defines SCR as "the adaptive capability of supply chain to be prepared for unexpected events and respond and recover to its original status [23]", then develops preparedness (§2.3.1), resistance (§2.3.2), rebound (§2.3.3) and growth (§2.3.4).
- §2.4 argues the alignment: "During preparedness, resilience requires early visibility, anticipation, and planning. Digital twins support these needs through end-to-end visibility, predictive insight, and scenario testing enabled by modelling and simulation, including what-if analysis."
- **[Assessment]** The conceptual sections are competent but the referencing is unreliable: the paper's own framework is attributed to four different sources across five locations, and at least three attributions are demonstrably wrong (scratchpad 5–9). For a review whose contribution *is* the framework, that matters.

## 5 METHODOLOGY
- **Approach (§3)**: SLR following Denyer & Tranfield's plan/execute/report cycle (Fig. 3), reported to PRISMA 2020 (Fig. 4; the PRISMA checklists are in the Supplementary Materials).
- **Databases (§3.1)**: Scopus and Web of Science. Google Scholar was tested — "It yielded 117 records; however, after deduplication using DOI matching and title and year checks, these records did not provide additional unique eligible studies" — and dropped.
- **Search strings (Table 2)**: `("digital twin" OR "digital shadow" OR "virtual twin") AND ("supply chain resilience" OR "supply chain uncertainty" OR "supply chain disruption" OR "robust supply chain")`, on WoS Topic and Scopus Title-Abstract-Keyword.
- **Inclusion**: peer-reviewed, English, substantive DTT-in-supply-chain focus, and explicit SCR/disruption/risk treatment *or* "extractable relevant resilience implications".
- **Exclusion**: grey literature (theses, reports, policy documents, white papers), non-English, no full text, conference reviews/editorials, passing mentions.
- **Screening (§3.2, Fig. 4)**: 242 → 182 (dedup) → 125 (title/abstract) → 99 (retrieval) → **89**. "To minimise bias, each study was independently evaluated by two researchers."
- **Quality appraisal (§3.2)**: "A formal quality appraisal was conducted using a light-touch checklist; accordingly, conclusions are phrased cautiously where evidence is primarily conceptual or where resilience impacts are implied rather than directly measured." The checklist itself is **not stated in paper**.
- **Coding (§4.2)**: "Full texts were coded manually in NVivo and Microsoft Excel using an abductive approach". **Two levels**: all 89 coded for benefits and challenges (Tables 6–7); a subset of 40 coded against the four stages (Table 5). Stage-assignment decision rules are stated ("Preparedness refers to ex ante sensing, planning, design, and stress testing before disruption", etc.).
- **Method taxonomy (§4.1.4)**: Wacker's theory-building vs empirical distinction, split into conceptual / analytical (mathematical, statistical) and empirical (experimental, statistical, case study).
- **[Assessment]** The search and screening are transparent and the PRISMA flow closes arithmetically — good. Three methodological weaknesses stand out: (a) the quality appraisal instrument is asserted but never shown, so "light-touch" cannot be evaluated; (b) inter-rater agreement between the "two researchers" is not reported (no kappa, no disagreement-resolution procedure); (c) the search string requires an SCR-phrase match, so digital-twin papers that address disruption without using those four exact phrases (e.g. "ripple effect", "viability", "risk propagation") are excluded by construction — a real limitation the paper does not flag, though it does flag database and language coverage in §7.2.

## 6 RESULTS
- **Publication trend (§4.1.1, Fig. 5)**: first article 2013; sporadic to 2018; 3 (2019), 13 (2020), 19 (2021), 18 (2022), 17 (2023), 5 (2024), 8 (2025, partial year to 31 May). "the strongest growth concentrated between 2019 and 2023."
- **Sectors (§4.1.2, Fig. 6)**: only 22 of 89 specify a sector — pharmaceutical 7, food 5, manufacturing 4, automotive 2, multi-sector 2, electricals 1, financial 1. "research on digital twins in supply-chain resilience is concentrated in a few critical industries."
- **Outlets (§4.1.3, Fig. 7)**: 34 journals; *IJPR* 9, *IFAC-PapersOnLine* 4, *Logistics* 3, then twos.
- **Methods (§4.1.4, Table 4)**: theory-building dominates — conceptual 24 + analytical 29 = 53, versus empirical 36, of which **only one is experimental**. "the relatively small share of experimental and empirical statistical studies suggests that rigorous large-scale testing of digital-twin-based resilience mechanisms remains limited."
- **Stage mapping (§4.2, Table 5, Fig. 8)**: preparedness 14, resistance 7, rebound 8, growth 11 articles; 15 capabilities in total. Preparedness = end-to-end visibility, predictive risk analytics, virtual stress testing, resilient network design. Resistance = real-time disruption detection, adaptive control, data-driven coordination, predictive maintenance. Rebound = predictive recovery modelling, recovery path evaluation, resource reconfiguration support, workflow automation. Growth = post-disruption analysis, capability development, strategic redesign.
- **Benefits (§4.3, Table 6)**: improve decision-making 21; real-time monitoring 15; risk reduction 15; improved efficiency 15; time and cost saving 10; predictive maintenance 8; supply-chain collaboration 8.
- **Barriers (§4.4, Table 7)**: data interoperability 5; data security and privacy 5; data accuracy and reliability 4; system compatibility 4; cost 3; model complexity 3; connectivity 2.
- **Boundary conditions (§5.4)**: "resilience effects are frequently inferred from improvements in visibility, prediction, or decision support instead of being measured through explicit resilience metrics"; "the term digital twin is used inconsistently across studies, ranging from episodic discrete event simulation models for scenario testing to continuously updated operational twins"; "reported benefits may be subject to publication bias and… the absence of negative case evidence should not be interpreted as the absence of failure risk."
- **[Assessment]** The counts are small and the barrier counts especially so — the largest barrier category rests on five articles. The paper's headline conclusions about "key barriers to adoption" are therefore descriptive tallies over a corpus in which most studies are conceptual, not evidence about how often those barriers bite in practice. §5.4 says as much, which is to the authors' credit, but the abstract does not.

## 8 DISCUSSION
- §5.1 answers RQ1 by restating the stage mapping with mechanisms: preparedness via visibility/critical-node identification/what-if testing; resistance via deviation detection, early warning and near-real-time mitigation simulation; rebound via recovery-path evaluation and time-to-recovery estimation, with the twin able to "replay events, assess which actions were most effective and refine recovery plans"; growth via DTT "as a learning infrastructure that accumulates knowledge from multiple disruption episodes".
- §5.3 answers RQ3 and adds practical prescriptions: harmonised data-integration policy and regulatory framework; scalable secure cloud; integration with blockchain, 5G, RFID, IoT; investment in sensor calibration and real-time synchronisation; and "investment in and cultivation of high precision technical talents".
- §5.4 is the paper's most valuable section — it explicitly lists the conditions under which the claims hold and states that "several capabilities, especially those associated with rebound and growth, should be treated as promising but still emerging propositions".
- §5.5 gives the DCV explanation: "Preparedness and resistance align more directly with sensing and seizing mechanisms, which are more readily demonstrated in existing studies. Rebound and growth rely more heavily on reconfiguring and learning mechanisms, which require deeper organisational embedding and therefore show more variable evidence across contexts."
- **[Assessment]** §5.4 and §5.5 together do something reviews often skip: they explain *why* the evidence is thinner where it is thin, rather than treating gaps as uniform. The explanation — preparedness and resistance can be demonstrated in simulation, rebound and growth require an operating twin and organisational embedding — is plausible and matches Table 4's design distribution.

## 9 LIMITATIONS
**Stated by the authors (§7.2):**
- "some relevant studies may not have been retrieved due to database coverage, indexing, and English-language restrictions, which may bias the sample towards well-represented outlets and regions."
- "only a subset of the included articles provided sufficient stage explicit detail for classification… the stage-based capability map may underrepresent mechanisms that are discussed without temporal specificity."
- "many studies were conceptual or simulation-based rather than evaluations of operational digital twins, meaning that resilience impacts are often inferred from improvements in visibility or decision support rather than measured through explicit resilience metrics."
- "implementation failures are likely underreported in published research, so the absence of negative case evidence should not be interpreted as absence of failure risk."
- §5.4 adds: dependence on enabling conditions (data availability, interoperability, governance); sector concentration in "food retail, semiconductors, and humanitarian logistics"; and inconsistent use of the term "digital twin".

**Not stated by the authors [Assessment]:**
- The search string requires one of four exact SCR phrases, excluding by construction the adjacent literature on ripple effect, viability and risk propagation that uses different terminology.
- No inter-rater reliability statistic is reported for the two-researcher screening, and no disagreement-resolution rule is given.
- The quality-appraisal checklist is named but never reproduced or applied visibly in Table 3.
- No effect sizes exist anywhere — the synthesis is entirely a count of how often a theme appears, and a theme appearing in 15 conceptual papers is reported the same way as one appearing in 15 empirical ones.
- Several arithmetic and attribution errors (scratchpad 1–15) that a reader cannot repair without the underlying coding sheet.
- §5.4 names "food retail, semiconductors, and humanitarian logistics" as the concentrated sectors, but Fig. 6 shows pharmaceutical, food and manufacturing. The two sector statements do not match.

## 10 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
- §7: "Research on digital twins in supply-chain resilience remains emergent." Two findings are stated as "well supported" — improved decision making through visibility/monitoring/modelling, and the data/interoperability/organisational-capability barrier cluster. Everything else is flagged as indicative.
- Six future-research directions (§6.1–6.6): explicit resilience theorising and metrics; **human–machine collaboration**; ethics (privacy, algorithmic bias, job displacement); cybersecurity and interoperability; sustainability (circular economy, Net Zero, digital product passports); and integration with blockchain/5G/IoT/robotics/VR/AR/AI.
- §6.2 in full, because it is the passage our paper relies on: "With digital twin models increasingly become more powerful, studies into how humans/managers can use the resulting information to make better decisions based on insights and recommendations generated by DTT are scarce. This is particularly true when DTT is integrated with AI technologies. DT in SCR heavily relies on AI and machine learning for data analysis and decision support. However, human expertise remains crucial for tasks requiring judgment, intuition, and understanding of the broader business context. Investigate how human expertise and decision-making can be effectively integrated with AI and machine learning algorithms is key to unlock DTT's full potential. This could involve research on user interface design, **trust, and transparency in AI-powered recommendations**, and the development of collaborative workflows for managing complex supply-chain scenarios [59]."
- **[Assessment]** §6.2 is, for our project, the most important 120 words in the paper. It is a 2026 systematic review of 89 studies stating that (a) research on how managers use twin-generated recommendations is scarce, and (b) the needed work is specifically on interface design, **trust** and **transparency in AI-powered recommendations**. That is a near-verbatim description of our study.

## 12 CRITICAL EVALUATION

**Strengths (max 3)**
1. **The stage framework is a real analytical contribution, and the capability/benefit/barrier separation is enforced.** §4.2 gives an explicit definition ("a DT-enabled functional mechanism that can be mobilised to support resilience") and explicit stage-assignment rules, and Fig. 8 renders the result cleanly. Most DTT reviews stop at "digital twins improve resilience"; this one says *which mechanism, when*.
2. **Unusually candid about the strength of its own evidence.** §5.4 and §7.2 state that most studies are conceptual or simulation-based, that resilience is inferred rather than measured, that reported benefits are subject to publication bias, and that rebound and growth claims are "promising but still emerging propositions". Table 4's "1 experimental study out of 89" is published rather than buried.
3. **The protocol is transparent enough to re-run.** Full search strings, both databases, itemised PRISMA exclusions that reconcile exactly, and an 89-row table of every included study with its research design.

**Weaknesses (max 3)**
1. **Internal arithmetic and citation errors in the load-bearing tables.** Table 5's Resistance row lists 8 references but counts 7; its four lists contain 32 distinct articles, not the 40 claimed in §4.2, and eight articles are double-listed despite a stated single-assignment rule. Table 6's "Predictive maintenance" lists 7 and counts 8, and Table 6 counts a Gartner Hype Cycle report ([109]) that the paper's own exclusion criteria bar, plus a seaport methods review ([108]). These are the tables that carry the contribution.
2. **The framework's provenance is attributed four different ways.** §2.3 credits the three stages to [1] and to [22]; Fig. 2 credits [24]; §4.2 credits [23] extended by [24]; Fig. 8 credits [23]; §2.3.4 credits growth to [1] while §4.2 credits it to [24]. Separately, [106]/[107] are transposed in §4.1.4 and [106] is cited for a NASA claim it has nothing to do with. For a review whose contribution is a synthesis of other people's frameworks, unreliable attribution is a substantive defect, not a typo.
3. **Vote-counting with no weighting by evidence quality.** Every number in Tables 5–7 is "how many papers mentioned this", with conceptual papers, simulations and case studies counted identically. The paper says it ran a quality appraisal but never shows the instrument or its output, so the reader cannot tell whether "risk reduction, 15 articles" rests on 15 demonstrations or 15 assertions. The largest barrier category rests on 5 articles.

**Credibility: MEDIUM** — the protocol, PRISMA flow and self-assessment of evidence strength are sound and re-runnable, but the synthesis tables carry miscounts, double-counted and out-of-scope references, and the central framework is attributed to four different sources across five locations; the qualitative conclusions are defensible, the counts are not quotable without checking.

**How much more this paper can carry than we currently ask of it**

We use `{liu}` twice. Three further passages are directly usable and would strengthen the paper's framing without any risk of over-claiming:

1. **§6.2 names our exact constructs.** The full sentence continues: "This could involve research on user interface design, **trust, and transparency in AI-powered recommendations**, and the development of collaborative workflows for managing complex supply-chain scenarios." Our study measures trust (Körber scale, `{korber}`), reliance on AI-powered recommendations, and works through a participant interface. Quoting or paraphrasing the fuller sentence makes the gap-to-study match explicit rather than leaving the reader to infer it.
2. **§5.4 supplies a published statement of the evidence gap our design fills**: "Many publications are conceptual, simulation based, or method development studies rather than evaluations of fully operational digital twins, meaning that resilience effects are frequently inferred from improvements in visibility, prediction, or decision support instead of being measured through explicit resilience metrics." Our study measures behaviour (weight of advice, RAIR/RSR) and outcome (stockout weeks, cost) rather than inferring them from a model's accuracy. This is a citable contrast.
3. **Table 4 is the quantitative form of the gap.** Of 89 digital-twin-and-resilience studies, **exactly one uses an experimental design**. That single number does more work for our introduction than the qualitative "scarce", and it is verifiable from a published table. Our first paragraph currently says only that research is "scarce"; "only one of 89 studies in a 2026 systematic review used an experimental design" is stronger, checkable and still exactly what the paper reports.
4. **§5.4's boundary condition matters for our own claims**: "the term digital twin is used inconsistently across studies, ranging from episodic discrete event simulation models for scenario testing to continuously updated operational twins". Our "digital twin" is a calibrated discrete-event simulation of a single-echelon system with no live data feed from a physical warehouse — the *first* of those two kinds. A reviewer from the operations-management community may well press on this. Citing §5.4 while stating plainly what our twin is (a parameter-calibrated simulation twin, not a sensor-fed operational twin) turns a possible objection into a disclosed scope condition. Our `build_paper.js:171` already describes the twin precisely enough; the addition would be one clause acknowledging the distinction.

**Where the paper does *not* support things we might be tempted to claim**
- It provides **no effect size** for any digital-twin benefit. Every number in Tables 5–7 is a count of papers mentioning a theme. We should never write anything of the form "digital twins improve X by Y% {liu}".
- Its "growth" stage and its DCV mapping (Fig. 9) are the authors' own extension, flagged by them as "promising but still emerging propositions" requiring longitudinal validation (§5.4). We should not lean on the four-stage framework as established theory.
- It says nothing about **human reliance, trust calibration, algorithm aversion or error placement** beyond the §6.2 call for research. It is evidence that the gap exists, not evidence about how people behave in it. Our behavioural framing must continue to rest on `{leesee,schemmer,dietvorst15,madhavan,parasuraman,bonaccio,schoeffer}`.
- Table 4's category is "experimental research (1)" in Wacker's sense, not "human-subjects experiment". Our claim should say "experimental design", matching the paper's own wording, rather than asserting that only one human-subjects experiment exists.

**Quality caveat to record**
This is an MDPI *Sustainability* systematic review with a 39-day submission-to-acceptance turnaround (received 20 Jan 2026, accepted 25 Feb 2026) and the internal miscounts and misattributions catalogued in §12. It is a perfectly serviceable citation for *the existence of a research gap* — that is a qualitative claim the paper supports directly and states in its own voice — and for the count of 89 studies and the 1-of-89 experimental design, both of which are internally verifiable (89 rows in Table 3; Table 4 sums to 89). It is **not** a source whose tabulated counts we should quote in aggregate, and if a reviewer challenges the citation, the defence is to point at §6.2 and Table 4 specifically rather than at the review as a whole.
